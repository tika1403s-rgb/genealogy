## 2. Field Nodes (Knowledge Traditions)

### Greek Traditions (5 nodes)

#### 2.1 Ionian Natural Inquiry
**Dates**: c. 600-400 BCE  
**Description**: Investigation of material principles (archai) underlying natural phenomena, initiated by Milesian thinkers who sought non-mythological explanations for the cosmos and natural change.  
**Significance**: Established rational inquiry into nature as distinct from mythological explanation; developed concept of underlying material principles.  
**Key figures**: Thales, Anaximander, Anaximenes, Heraclitus  
**Sources**: Laks & Most (2016), Graham (2010)

#### 2.2 Pluralist Material Theory
**Dates**: c. 500-400 BCE  
**Description**: Theories positing multiple fundamental substances or elements as constituents of matter, including four-element and seed theories.  
**Significance**: Moved beyond monistic explanations; developed sophisticated accounts of material composition and change.  
**Key figures**: Empedocles, Anaxagoras  
**Sources**: Graham (2010), Lloyd (1970)

#### 2.3 Greek Atomism
**Dates**: c. 460-270 BCE  
**Description**: Theory that all matter consists of indivisible particles (atomoi) moving in void space, with macroscopic properties emerging from atomic combinations.  
**Significance**: First comprehensive mechanistic account of matter; influenced later Epicurean physics and modern atomic theory.  
**Key figures**: Leucippus, Democritus, Epicurus  
**Sources**: Berryman (2009), Ancient Atomism (Stanford)

#### 2.4 Peripatetic Natural Philosophy
**Dates**: c. 350-200 BCE  
**Description**: Aristotle's systematic investigation of nature emphasizing observation, classification, teleology, and principles of change in living and non-living things.  
**Significance**: Established systematic biology, formal logic, and comprehensive natural philosophy; dominated Western thought for centuries.  
**Key figures**: Aristotle, Theophrastus, Strato of Lampsacus  
**Sources**: Lloyd (1970), Naddaf (2005)

#### 2.5 Alexandrian Mathematical Sciences
**Dates**: c. 300-100 BCE  
**Description**: Systematic mathematical investigation of geometry, astronomy, mechanics, and optics centered at Alexandria's Museum and Library.  
**Significance**: Developed axiomatic method, advanced pure mathematics, applied mathematics to natural phenomena.  
**Key figures**: Euclid, Archimedes, Apollonius, Eratosthenes  
**Sources**: Russo (2004), Heath (1921)

### Chinese Traditions (4 nodes)

#### 2.6 Mohist Investigation
**Dates**: c. 470-221 BCE  
**Description**: Systematic study of optics, mechanics, logic, and definitions developed by followers of Mozi, including experimental observations and geometric analysis.  
**Significance**: Earliest Chinese tradition of systematic natural investigation; documented optical phenomena, mechanical principles, and logical definitions.  
**Key figures**: Mozi, Later Mohist thinkers  
**Sources**: Graham (1978), Needham (1956)

#### 2.7 Yin-Yang and Five Phases School
**Dates**: c. 350-100 BCE  
**Description**: Systematic cosmological framework explaining natural change through complementary principles (yin/yang) and cyclic transformations of five elemental phases.  
**Significance**: Provided unified explanatory framework for astronomy, medicine, and natural cycles; influenced all subsequent Chinese natural investigation.  
**Key figures**: Zou Yan, associated Jixia Academy scholars  
**Sources**: Needham (1956), Fung Yu-lan (1952)

#### 2.8 Daoist Natural Observation
**Dates**: c. 400 BCE-200 CE  
**Description**: Emphasis on careful observation of natural patterns, spontaneous processes, and transformation; later incorporated alchemical experimentation.  
**Significance**: Promoted naturalistic observation free from human intervention paradigms; fostered proto-chemical experimentation.  
**Key figures**: Laozi, Zhuangzi, Daoist alchemists  
**Sources**: Needham (1956), Science and Chinese Philosophy (Stanford)

### Indian Traditions (3 nodes)

#### 2.9 Vaiśeṣika Atomism
**Dates**: c. 600 BCE-200 CE  
**Description**: Systematic investigation of reality through categories (padārthas) including atomic theory of matter composed of indivisible particles (paramāṇu) of earth, water, fire, and air.  
**Significance**: Developed sophisticated atomistic metaphysics; integrated natural philosophy with epistemology and liberation doctrine.  
**Key figures**: Kaṇāda, Praśastapāda  
**Sources**: Gangopadhyaya (1980), Subbarayappa (1967)

#### 2.10 Nyāya Logic and Natural Inquiry
**Dates**: c. 400 BCE-200 CE  
**Description**: Investigation of valid means of knowledge (pramāṇas) applied to understanding natural world; developed formal logic and inference methods.  
**Significance**: Established rigorous epistemology for natural investigation; later merged with Vaiśeṣika into unified system.  
**Key figures**: Gautama (Akṣapāda), Vātsyāyana  
**Sources**: Matilal (1986), Gangopadhyaya (1980)

#### 2.11 Buddhist Phenomenological Atomism
**Dates**: c. 200 BCE-400 CE  
**Description**: Analysis of momentary micro-events (dharmas) and material atoms emphasizing transience; developed phenomenological approach to material reality.  
**Significance**: Integrated temporal atomism with spatial atomism; critiqued substance-based metaphysics.  
**Key figures**: Dharmaśrī, Vasubandhu, Saṅghabhadra  
**Sources**: Ronkin (2005), Sarkar (2021)

#### 2.12 Jaina Atomism and Material Theory
**Dates**: c. 500 BCE-200 CE  
**Description**: Theory of pudgala (matter) composed of indivisible atoms (paramāṇu) forming aggregates (skandhas); single homogenous matter undergoing fusion and fission.  
**Significance**: Distinct from Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika by positing homogenous matter rather than element-specific atoms; integrated with Jaina karma theory.  
**Key figures**: Umāsvāti, early Jaina ācāryas  
**Sources**: Gangopadhyaya (1980), Ancient Atomism (Stanford)

### Roman Tradition (1 node)

#### 2.13 Roman Natural History
**Dates**: c. 50 BCE-200 CE  
**Description**: Encyclopedic compilation and practical investigation of animals, plants, minerals, and natural phenomena; emphasized observation and utility.  
**Significance**: Preserved and transmitted Greek learning; documented vast range of natural phenomena for practical application.  
**Key figures**: Varro, Lucretius, Pliny the Elder, Galen  
**Sources**: French & Greenaway (1986)

## 3. Pioneers (30-40 individuals)

### Greek/Hellenistic Pioneers (18)

#### 3.1 Thales of Miletus
**Dates**: c. 624-546 BCE  
**Contributions**: Proposed water as fundamental principle (archē) of all things; predicted solar eclipse; attempted natural explanations for earthquakes.  
**Significance**: Traditionally regarded as first philosopher to seek natural rather than mythological explanations for phenomena.  
**Sources**: Graham (2010), Lloyd (1970)

#### 3.2 Anaximander of Miletus
**Dates**: c. 610-546 BCE  
**Contributions**: Proposed the apeiron (unlimited/indefinite) as cosmic principle; developed model of Earth suspended in space; described evolution-like origins of life from moisture.  
**Significance**: Advanced abstract theorizing beyond observable substances; pioneered cosmological models.  
**Sources**: Graham (2010), Naddaf (2005)

#### 3.3 Anaximenes of Miletus
**Dates**: c. 586-526 BCE  
**Contributions**: Proposed air as fundamental substance; explained condensation and rarefaction as mechanisms of change; attempted systematic cosmology.  
**Significance**: Developed first mechanistic account of material transformation.  
**Sources**: Graham (2010)

#### 3.4 Heraclitus of Ephesus
**Dates**: c. 535-475 BCE  
**Contributions**: Proposed fire as cosmic principle; emphasized perpetual flux and change; developed logos concept as rational principle governing change.  
**Significance**: Established change itself as fundamental reality; influenced dialectical thinking.  
**Sources**: Graham (2010), Lloyd (1970)

#### 3.5 Parmenides of Elea
**Dates**: c. 515-450 BCE  
**Contributions**: Argued for unchanging Being through logical analysis; developed cosmological model; pioneered logical argumentation in natural philosophy.  
**Significance**: Established logical requirements for valid accounts of nature; provoked responses leading to atomism and pluralism.  
**Sources**: Graham (2010), Berryman (2009)

#### 3.6 Empedocles of Acragas
**Dates**: c. 494-434 BCE  
**Contributions**: Proposed four elements (earth, water, air, fire) as roots of all things; developed theory of cosmic cycles driven by Love and Strife; investigated respiration and blood.  
**Significance**: Established influential four-element theory; attempted mechanistic biology.  
**Sources**: Graham (2010), Lloyd (1970)

#### 3.7 Anaxagoras of Clazomenae
**Dates**: c. 500-428 BCE  
**Contributions**: Proposed infinite number of elemental seeds (spermata); explained moon's light as reflection; investigated meteors and comets.  
**Significance**: Advanced pluralistic material theory; brought philosophy to Athens; influenced Aristotle.  
**Sources**: Graham (2010), Lloyd (1970)

#### 3.8 Leucippus
**Dates**: flourished c. 440 BCE  
**Contributions**: Originated atomistic theory with indivisible particles (atoms) moving in void; proposed mechanistic causation.  
**Significance**: Founded atomism as systematic natural philosophy; established void as necessary for motion.  
**Sources**: Ancient Atomism (Stanford), Berryman (2009)

#### 3.9 Democritus of Abdera
**Dates**: c. 460-370 BCE  
**Contributions**: Systematized atomic theory; explained sensory qualities as atomic configurations; developed atomic psychology; investigated mathematics and astronomy.  
**Significance**: Most comprehensive ancient atomist; influenced Epicurean school and modern atomic theory.  
**Sources**: Ancient Atomism (Stanford), Graham (2010)

#### 3.10 Hippocrates of Kos
**Dates**: c. 460-370 BCE  
**Contributions**: Developed systematic observational medicine; emphasized natural causes of disease; established medical ethics; authored or inspired Hippocratic Corpus.  
**Significance**: Established rational medicine based on observation and theory; separated medicine from religious practice.  
**Sources**: Lloyd (1970)

#### 3.11 Plato
**Dates**: c. 428-348 BCE  
**Contributions**: Developed geometrical atomism in Timaeus; investigated mathematical forms and their relation to nature; established Academy.  
**Significance**: Integrated mathematics with natural philosophy; influenced Aristotle and later Neoplatonism.  
**Sources**: Lloyd (1970), Ancient Atomism (Stanford)

#### 3.12 Aristotle of Stagira
**Dates**: 384-322 BCE  
**Contributions**: Developed systematic natural philosophy covering physics, biology, psychology, meteorology; established Lyceum; classified hundreds of animal species.  
**Significance**: Created comprehensive philosophical system integrating all natural knowledge; dominated Western thought until modern period.  
**Sources**: Lloyd (1970), Naddaf (2005)

#### 3.13 Theophrastus of Eresus
**Dates**: c. 371-287 BCE  
**Contributions**: Succeeded Aristotle; systematically investigated plants (Enquiry into Plants); studied mineralogy and meteorology.  
**Significance**: Established botany as systematic discipline; preserved and extended Aristotelian tradition.  
**Sources**: Lloyd (1970)

#### 3.14 Euclid of Alexandria
**Dates**: flourished c. 300 BCE  
**Contributions**: Compiled and systematized geometry in Elements; developed axiomatic method; contributed to optics and astronomy.  
**Significance**: Established model for mathematical proof; Elements remained standard for 2000+ years.  
**Sources**: Heath (1921), Russo (2004)

#### 3.15 Archimedes of Syracuse
**Dates**: c. 287-212 BCE  
**Contributions**: Developed principles of hydrostatics and levers; calculated π; invented mechanical devices; advanced mathematical methods.  
**Significance**: Greatest ancient mathematician-engineer; established mathematical mechanics.  
**Sources**: Russo (2004), Heath (1921)

#### 3.16 Eratosthenes of Cyrene
**Dates**: c. 276-194 BCE  
**Contributions**: Calculated Earth's circumference; created star catalog; developed geographical mapping methods.  
**Significance**: Applied mathematical methods to measure Earth; pioneered mathematical geography.  
**Sources**: Russo (2004)

#### 3.17 Aristarchus of Samos
**Dates**: c. 310-230 BCE  
**Contributions**: Proposed heliocentric model with Earth revolving around Sun; attempted to measure distances to Sun and Moon.  
**Significance**: First known heliocentric theory; 1800 years before Copernicus.  
**Sources**: Russo (2004)

#### 3.18 Apollonius of Perga
**Dates**: c. 262-190 BCE  
**Contributions**: Developed comprehensive theory of conic sections; advanced mathematical astronomy; refined geometrical methods.  
**Significance**: Established foundation for later astronomical models and analytical geometry.  
**Sources**: Heath (1921), Russo (2004)

### Chinese Pioneers (10)

#### 3.19 Mozi (Mo Di)
**Dates**: c. 470-391 BCE  
**Contributions**: Developed logical definitions; emphasized universal love and utility; established Mohist school focusing on practical knowledge.  
**Significance**: Founded Chinese tradition of systematic logical and natural investigation.  
**Sources**: Graham (1978), Needham (1956)

#### 3.20 Later Mohist Authors
**Dates**: c. 350-250 BCE  
**Contributions**: Documented optical phenomena including aperture imaging and mirror reflection; developed geometric and mechanical definitions; analyzed logical paradoxes.  
**Significance**: Produced most systematic ancient Chinese investigation of optics and mechanics.  
**Sources**: Graham (1978), Needham (1956)

#### 3.21 Zou Yan
**Dates**: c. 305-240 BCE  
**Contributions**: Systematized yin-yang theory with Five Phases (wuxing); applied cosmological cycles to history; taught at Jixia Academy.  
**Significance**: Called "real founder of all Chinese scientific thought" by Needham; established framework for all subsequent Chinese natural investigation.  
**Sources**: Needham (1956), Fung Yu-lan (1952)

#### 3.22 Xunzi
**Dates**: c. 310-235 BCE  
**Contributions**: Developed naturalistic philosophy emphasizing observation; distinguished natural patterns from human values; critiqued superstition.  
**Significance**: Advanced empirical approach to understanding nature separate from moral concerns.  
**Sources**: Fung Yu-lan (1952)

#### 3.23 Hui Shi
**Dates**: c. 380-305 BCE  
**Contributions**: Developed relativistic paradoxes; emphasized continuous change; explored concepts of space, time, and magnitude.  
**Significance**: Advanced dialectical thinking about natural phenomena; influenced later logicians.  
**Sources**: Fung Yu-lan (1952)

#### 3.24 Gongsun Long
**Dates**: c. 325-250 BCE  
**Contributions**: Developed logical analyses of universals and particulars; argued for permanence and absolute qualities; studied language and reality.  
**Significance**: Advanced Chinese logical investigation; debated with Hui Shi about nature of reality.  
**Sources**: Fung Yu-lan (1952)

#### 3.25 Shen Kuo (included for influence though later)
**Note**: While Shen Kuo (1031-1095 CE) is outside our period, earlier Daoist alchemists and natural observers (c. 200 BCE-200 CE) contributed to observational traditions.  
**Contributions**: Proto-Daoist investigators observed natural transformations, documented mineral and chemical changes.  
**Significance**: Established tradition of careful natural observation and proto-experimental method.  
**Sources**: Needham (1956)

#### 3.26 Bian Que
**Dates**: c. 400-310 BCE  
**Contributions**: Developed systematic diagnostic methods; emphasized diet and preventive medicine; may have compiled medical texts.  
**Significance**: Advanced Chinese medical observation and theory.  
**Sources**: Fung Yu-lan (1952)

### Indian Pioneers (8)

#### 3.27 Kaṇāda (Kashyapa)
**Dates**: c. 600-200 BCE (dates uncertain)  
**Contributions**: Composed Vaiśeṣika Sūtra establishing atomic theory; developed category system (padārthas); investigated material composition.  
**Significance**: Founded Vaiśeṣika school; established Indian atomism as systematic philosophy.  
**Sources**: Gangopadhyaya (1980), Subbarayappa (1967)

#### 3.28 Gautama (Akṣapāda)
**Dates**: c. 400-200 BCE  
**Contributions**: Composed Nyāya Sūtra establishing logic and epistemology; developed inference methods; analyzed sources of knowledge.  
**Significance**: Founded Nyāya school; established rigorous epistemology for natural investigation.  
**Sources**: Matilal (1986)

#### 3.29 Praśastapāda
**Dates**: c. 500-600 CE (later but influential on tradition)  
**Contributions**: Wrote Padārthadharmasaṅgraha, major commentary on Vaiśeṣika; elaborated atomic theory and categories.  
**Significance**: Systematized and clarified Vaiśeṣika atomism for later tradition.  
**Sources**: Gangopadhyaya (1980)

#### 3.30 Vasubandhu
**Dates**: c. 400-480 CE  
**Contributions**: Composed Abhidharmakośabhāṣya presenting Buddhist atomism; later critiqued atomism in Twenty Verses (Viṃśatikā); developed phenomenological approach.  
**Significance**: Most influential Buddhist philosopher on atomism; advanced phenomenological alternatives.  
**Sources**: Ronkin (2005), Sarkar (2021)

#### 3.31 Dharmaśrī
**Dates**: c. 200 CE  
**Contributions**: First Buddhist thinker to systematically develop material atomism in Abhidharma tradition.  
**Significance**: Established Buddhist atomistic metaphysics distinct from Vaiśeṣika version.  
**Sources**: Ronkin (2005)

#### 3.32 Saṅghabhadra
**Dates**: c. 400-500 CE  
**Contributions**: Developed Sarvāstivāda atomism; analyzed relationship between atoms and aggregates.  
**Significance**: Advanced realistic atomism within Buddhist metaphysics.  
**Sources**: Ronkin (2005)

#### 3.33 Dharmakīrti
**Dates**: c. 600-660 CE (slightly outside period but foundational)  
**Contributions**: Wrote Pramāṇavārttika on valid knowledge; developed Buddhist epistemology; analyzed perception and inference; contributed to atomistic theory.  
**Significance**: Most influential Buddhist epistemologist; integrated logic with atomistic metaphysics.  
**Sources**: Ancient Atomism (Stanford)

### Roman Pioneers (4)

#### 3.34 Marcus Terentius Varro
**Dates**: 116-27 BCE  
**Contributions**: Composed Res Rusticae on agriculture; wrote extensively on geography, biology, mathematics (mostly lost); influenced later Roman encyclopedists.  
**Significance**: Most prolific Roman scientific author; transmitted Greek learning to Latin tradition.  
**Sources**: French & Greenaway (1986)

#### 3.35 Titus Lucretius Carus
**Dates**: c. 99-55 BCE  
**Contributions**: Composed De Rerum Natura expounding Epicurean atomism; investigated optics, biology, psychology in poetic form.  
**Significance**: Preserved and transmitted Greek atomism; influenced Renaissance revival of atomism.  
**Sources**: Ancient Atomism (Stanford)

#### 3.36 Pliny the Elder (Gaius Plinius Secundus)
**Dates**: 23-79 CE  
**Contributions**: Compiled 37-volume Naturalis Historia covering astronomy, geography, zoology, botany, mineralogy, medicine; cited 2000+ sources.  
**Significance**: Created first scientific encyclopedia; preserved vast ancient knowledge; standard reference for centuries.  
**Sources**: French & Greenaway (1986)

#### 3.37 Galen of Pergamon
**Dates**: 129-216 CE  
**Contributions**: Systematized medical knowledge; performed anatomical investigations; developed humoral theory; served as imperial physician.  
**Significance**: Most influential ancient physician; medical authority until modern period.  
**Sources**: French & Greenaway (1986)

## 4. Breakthroughs (Enabling Inventions/Concepts)

### Conceptual Breakthroughs (8)

#### 4.1 Natural Explanation Paradigm
**Dates**: c. 600-500 BCE  
**Description**: Shift from mythological to natural explanations for phenomena; rejection of divine intervention as primary cause.  
**Significance**: Foundational conceptual breakthrough enabling all subsequent natural investigation.  
**Tradition**: Greek (Milesian school)  
**Sources**: Graham (2010), Naddaf (2005)

#### 4.2 Material Archē Concept
**Dates**: c. 600-400 BCE  
**Description**: Idea that single or multiple underlying material principles (archai) constitute all things and explain change.  
**Significance**: Established material monism and pluralism as investigative frameworks.  
**Tradition**: Greek (Ionian)  
**Sources**: Graham (2010)

#### 4.3 Atomic Theory of Matter
**Dates**: c. 440-270 BCE (Greek); c. 600 BCE-200 CE (Indian)  
**Description**: Theory that matter consists of indivisible particles separated by void, with properties emerging from atomic arrangements.  
**Significance**: First mechanistic account of matter; parallel development in Greece and India.  
**Traditions**: Greek (Leucippus, Democritus), Indian (Kaṇāda, Buddhist schools)  
**Sources**: Ancient Atomism (Stanford), Gangopadhyaya (1980)

#### 4.4 Axiomatic Method
**Dates**: c. 300 BCE  
**Description**: Deduction of theorems from small set of axioms and postulates using logical inference.  
**Significance**: Established model for mathematical proof; transformed how knowledge was organized and validated.  
**Tradition**: Greek (Euclid)  
**Sources**: Heath (1921), Russo (2004)

#### 4.5 Yin-Yang and Five Phases Framework
**Dates**: c. 350-200 BCE  
**Description**: Systematic cosmological model explaining change through complementary principles and cyclic elemental transformations.  
**Significance**: Unified framework for understanding natural cycles, medicine, astronomy; dominated East Asian thought.  
**Tradition**: Chinese (Zou Yan and predecessors)  
**Sources**: Needham (1956), Fung Yu-lan (1952)

#### 4.6 Category Systems (Padārtha)
**Dates**: c. 600-200 BCE  
**Description**: Systematic classification of reality into fundamental categories (substance, quality, action, etc.).  
**Significance**: Established organized ontology for investigating nature; integrated epistemology and metaphysics.  
**Tradition**: Indian (Vaiśeṣika, Nyāya)  
**Sources**: Gangopadhyaya (1980), Matilal (1986)

#### 4.7 Systematic Biological Classification
**Dates**: c. 350-320 BCE  
**Description**: Hierarchical organization of living things by similarities and differences; emphasis on observation and description.  
**Significance**: Established biology as systematic science; model for classification lasting until Linnaeus.  
**Tradition**: Greek (Aristotle)  
**Sources**: Lloyd (1970)

#### 4.8 Valid Knowledge Instruments (Pramāṇa Theory)
**Dates**: c. 400 BCE-200 CE  
**Description**: Systematic analysis of perception, inference, comparison, and testimony as means of acquiring reliable knowledge about nature.  
**Significance**: Established epistemological foundation for natural investigation; rigorous analysis of knowledge sources.  
**Tradition**: Indian (Nyāya, Buddhist schools)  
**Sources**: Matilal (1986)

### Technological/Methodological Breakthroughs (7)

#### 4.9 Mathematical Proof Techniques
**Dates**: c. 400-300 BCE  
**Description**: Development of geometric proof methods including reductio ad absurdum, construction proofs, and exhaustion method.  
**Significance**: Enabled rigorous mathematical investigation of natural phenomena.  
**Tradition**: Greek (Eudoxus, Euclid)  
**Sources**: Heath (1921)

#### 4.10 Precise Astronomical Measurement
**Dates**: c. 300-200 BCE  
**Description**: Development of instruments and techniques for measuring celestial positions, calculating planetary motions, and determining Earth's dimensions.  
**Significance**: Enabled quantitative astronomy and geography; Eratosthenes measured Earth's circumference.  
**Tradition**: Hellenistic (Alexandria)  
**Sources**: Russo (2004)

#### 4.11 Systematic Optical Observation
**Dates**: c. 350-250 BCE  
**Description**: Documentation of reflection, refraction, aperture imaging, and mirror properties through observation and geometric analysis.  
**Significance**: Earliest systematic investigation of optical phenomena.  
**Tradition**: Chinese (Mohist school)  
**Sources**: Graham (1978), Needham (1956)

#### 4.12 Hydrostatic and Mechanical Principles
**Dates**: c. 250-212 BCE  
**Description**: Archimedes' principle of buoyancy; lever principle; mathematical analysis of mechanical advantage.  
**Significance**: Established mathematical mechanics; practical applications in engineering.  
**Tradition**: Hellenistic (Archimedes)  
**Sources**: Russo (2004)

#### 4.13 Encyclopedic Compilation Method
**Dates**: c. 50 BCE-100 CE  
**Description**: Systematic organization of diverse knowledge with citations; comprehensive coverage of natural phenomena.  
**Significance**: Preserved and transmitted knowledge across civilizations; model for later encyclopedias.  
**Tradition**: Roman (Varro, Pliny)  
**Sources**: French & Greenaway (1986)

#### 4.14 Clinical Medical Observation
**Dates**: c. 400 BCE-200 CE  
**Description**: Systematic recording of symptoms, disease progression, treatments, and outcomes; emphasis on prognosis based on observation.  
**Significance**: Established observational medicine; separated medicine from religious practice.  
**Traditions**: Greek (Hippocratic school), Chinese (Yellow Emperor tradition), Indian (Ayurvedic)  
**Sources**: Lloyd (1970), Needham (1956)

#### 4.15 Logical Paradox Analysis
**Dates**: c. 400-250 BCE  
**Description**: Investigation of apparent contradictions regarding motion, space, time, and divisibility to clarify concepts.  
**Significance**: Sharpened analytical tools; provoked development of atomism and sophisticated accounts of change.  
**Traditions**: Greek (Zeno of Elea, Eleatic school), Chinese (Hui Shi, School of Names)  
**Sources**: Graham (2010), Fung Yu-lan (1952)

## 5. Critical Works (15-20 Key Texts)

### Greek Works (8)

#### 5.1 Fragments of Early Greek Philosophy
**Author**: Various (Thales, Anaximander, Anaximenes, Heraclitus, others)  
**Dates**: c. 600-450 BCE (original composition); surviving as fragments in later authors  
**Description**: Brief surviving passages and testimonia from Milesian and Ephesian thinkers on material principles and cosmology.  
**Significance**: Earliest Greek natural philosophy; established inquiry into physis (nature).  
**Sources**: Laks & Most (2016), Graham (2010)

#### 5.2 Fragments of Leucippus and Democritus
**Authors**: Leucippus, Democritus  
**Dates**: c. 440-370 BCE (original); surviving as fragments  
**Works**: Democritus' Great World-System, Little World-System, On the Mind (fragments)  
**Description**: Development of atomic theory with atoms and void as fundamental realities.  
**Significance**: Foundational texts of ancient atomism; influenced Epicurus and later atomists.  
**Sources**: Ancient Atomism (Stanford), Graham (2010)

#### 5.3 Hippocratic Corpus
**Authors**: Various (attributed to Hippocrates and followers)  
**Dates**: c. 430-200 BCE  
**Key works**: On Ancient Medicine, On the Nature of Man, Airs Waters Places, Prognostic

**Significance**: Established rational medicine; influenced all subsequent Western medical tradition.  
**Sources**: Lloyd (1970)

#### 5.4 Physics (Physica)
**Author**: Aristotle  
**Dates**: c. 350-330 BCE

**Significance**: Foundational text of natural philosophy; dominated physics until early modern period.  
**Sources**: Lloyd (1970), Naddaf (2005)

#### 5.5 On the Soul (De Anima)
**Author**: Aristotle  
**Dates**: c. 350 BCE

**Sources**: Lloyd (1970)

**Author**: Aristotle

**Sources**: Lloyd (1970)

#### 5.7 Elements (Stoicheia)
**Author**: Euclid  
**Dates**: c. 300 BCE

**Significance**: Most influential mathematics text ever written; defined mathematical rigor for 2000+ years.  
**Sources**: Heath (1921), Russo (2004)

#### 5.8 Works of Archimedes
**Author**: Archimedes  
**Dates**: c. 250-212 BCE  
**Key works**: On the Sphere and Cylinder, On Floating Bodies, Measurement of a Circle, The Sand Reckoner

**Significance**: Pinnacle of ancient mathematical physics; established mathematical approach to nature.  
**Sources**: Heath (1921), Russo (2004)

**Sources**: French & Greenaway (1986)
